# The Ripple — A Choice-Based Story Game Series for Kids

**Audience:** Ages 8–12
**Format:** Interactive web-based branching narrative games
**Core idea:** Every choice creates ripples. Good choices open doors. Bad choices narrow the path. Doubling down on bad choices makes things worse. But owning up — even late — can start recovery.

---

## Series Overview

"The Ripple" is an umbrella brand for a series of short, replayable story games. Each game explores one core value through everyday scenarios a kid would actually face. The player makes choices, and each choice shapes what happens next — and what options are even available.

All games share the same engine and mechanics but tell different stories with different themes.

## Shared Game Mechanics

These mechanics are common across all games in the series.

### The Meter (0–100)

Each game has a themed meter that starts at 50. It moves based on choices.

| Range | Label | Effect |
|-------|-------|--------|
| 80–100 | Excellent | Bonus options unlock, NPCs are warm and supportive |
| 50–79 | Okay | Standard options, normal NPC behavior |
| 30–49 | Shaky | Fewer good options, NPCs are wary, recovery is harder |
| 15–29 | Critical | Damage starts becoming permanent, some relationships break |
| 0–14 | Broken | Point of no return for some storylines |

### The Compounding Rule

This is the heart of the game. Choices don't exist in isolation — they build on each other.

**Compounding downward:** A single bad choice (like a small lie) costs maybe -5 on the meter. But if the next choice doubles down on it (lie again to cover the first lie), the penalty is -12, not -5. A third escalation might be -20. The cost accelerates because the situation gets more tangled.

**Recovery windows:** After a bad choice, the very next decision usually includes a "come clean" or "own up" option. Taking it costs something (embarrassment, a small penalty) but stops the bleeding and opens a recovery path. Ignoring the recovery window makes the next recovery harder and more expensive.

**Compounding upward:** Good choices also compound. Being honest once is +5. Being honest in a harder situation after that is +8. Consistently good choices unlock "bonus" scenarios — moments where the player's built-up goodwill pays off in unexpected ways (someone defends them, a teacher gives them a second chance they didn't ask for).

### The Threshold

There is a threshold below which certain consequences become permanent for that storyline. A friendship might break. A teacher might stop trusting the player entirely. This isn't game over — the game continues — but it teaches that some damage can't be undone. The threshold is at 15.

The game is honest about this. When it happens, the narrative says something like: "Riya doesn't want to talk to you anymore. Some things, once broken, stay broken." This isn't punitive — it's a real lesson delivered gently.

### Choice Design

Every decision point offers 2–3 options. They are NOT labeled good/bad. Instead, they're written as things a kid might genuinely consider:

- The honest/brave option (clearly right but uncomfortable)
- The avoidant/neutral option (not terrible, but doesn't fix things)
- The selfish/dishonest option (easier in the moment but costly later)

Subtle color hints (warm green, amber, soft red) can guide younger players, but the choices should be written clearly enough that kids can feel the weight of each option on their own.

### The Journal

As the player progresses, a "journal" records what happened in plain language:

> "You told Mrs. Sharma that Riya broke the phone. But Riya wasn't even near the table. Now Riya is upset, and Mrs. Sharma isn't sure who to believe."

At the end, the player can read their full story. This is powerful for replay — they can compare journals from different runs and see how different choices led to different stories.

### End States

Each game ends with a summary based on the final meter value. There are 4 tiers:

- **Excellent (80+):** A warm, affirming ending. Relationships are strong, the player is trusted and respected. Message: "Your choices built something real."
- **Good (50–79):** A realistic ending. Some mistakes were made but lessons were learned. Message: "You stumbled, but you got back up. That's what matters."
- **Rocky (30–49):** A sobering ending. Things are strained. Trust is thin. Message: "It's not too late — but it's getting harder. What will you choose tomorrow?"
- **Broken (below 30):** A hard ending, but not cruel. Consequences are real. Message: "Some ripples can't be taken back. But every day is a new stone to throw."

All endings include a "Play Again?" option and a prompt: "What would you do differently?"

## Game 1: Tangled (Trust & Honesty)

### Setting

Alex is a 10-year-old starting the second term at school. They have a small friend group — Riya (loyal, direct), Sam (funny, easily influenced), and Priya Ma'am (their class teacher, fair but firm).

### Scenario Chains

The game has 4 scenario chains, played in sequence. Each chain is 3–4 decisions deep. The meter carries across all chains, so early choices affect late-game options.

#### Chain 1: The Broken Phone

Alex accidentally knocks Riya's phone off the lunch table and cracks the screen.

**Round 1 — The moment it happens:**
- "I'm so sorry, I knocked it. Let me talk to my parents about fixing it." → Trust +8
- "Oh no, it fell! I think it was already on the edge." → Trust -3
- "I didn't touch it — I think Sam bumped the table." → Trust -8

**Round 2 (if blamed Sam) — Sam says he wasn't near the table. Priya Ma'am looks at Alex:**
- "Actually… I think it was me. I'm sorry I said that." → Trust -2 net (partial recovery, Sam is relieved)
- "Sam probably didn't notice he did it." → Trust -12 (doubling down, Sam is hurt)
- Stay quiet, look at the floor. → Trust -6 (avoidant, teacher is suspicious)

**Round 3 (if doubled down) — Sam is upset. Riya is confused. Other kids saw what happened:**
- Find Sam at recess: "I messed up. I'm really sorry. It was me." → Trust +4 (hard recovery, slow rebuild)
- Tell another friend: "Sam's overreacting, it's not a big deal." → Trust -18 (escalation, crosses threshold risk)
- Avoid everyone at lunch. → Trust -5 (isolation, no recovery)

**Round 4 (if escalated) — Riya confronts Alex directly: "I know you broke my phone. Everyone saw it."**
- "You're right. I broke it and I lied. I'm sorry." → Trust +3 (late recovery, but Riya appreciates honesty; Sam friendship damaged but not lost)
- "Whatever, it's just a phone." → Trust -15 (Riya friendship permanently damaged if below threshold)

#### Chain 2: The Group Project

Alex is in a group project with Riya, Sam, and a quiet kid named Kabir. Kabir does most of the research but is too shy to present.

**Round 1 — Presenting the project:**
- Credit everyone, especially Kabir: "Kabir did amazing research, let me show what he found." → Trust +7
- Present it as a group effort without naming anyone specific. → Trust +2
- Take most of the credit: "I put this together. The others helped a bit." → Trust -8

**Round 2 (if took credit) — Kabir looks hurt. After class, Priya Ma'am asks who did what:**
- Be honest: "Kabir did most of the research. I should have said that." → Trust +5 (recovery)
- Minimize it: "We all did equal work." → Trust -6
- Double down: "I organized everything. Kabir just googled some stuff." → Trust -14 (Kabir withdraws, group trust broken)

**Round 3 (if honest/recovery) — Priya Ma'am asks Alex to acknowledge Kabir in front of the class:**
- Do it genuinely. → Trust +6 (Kabir smiles, friendship forms)
- Do it but make it awkward/minimal. → Trust +1 (obligation fulfilled but not felt)

#### Chain 3: The Secret

Riya tells Alex privately that her parents are getting separated. She's upset and asks Alex not to tell anyone.

**Round 1 — At lunch, Sam asks "What's wrong with Riya today?"**
- "She's going through something. She'll talk about it when she's ready." → Trust +8
- "I don't know, she seemed fine to me." → Trust -2 (small lie, protective intent)
- "Her parents are splitting up. Don't tell anyone." → Trust -12 (betrayal of confidence)

**Round 2 (if told Sam) — Sam accidentally mentions it to someone else. It spreads. Riya finds out Alex told:**
- Go to Riya immediately: "I told Sam. I shouldn't have. I'm so sorry." → Trust -4 net (damage done but ownership shown)
- "I only told Sam, he's the one who spread it." → Trust -10 (blame shifting)
- Avoid Riya. → Trust -6 (avoidant)

**Round 3 (if blame-shifted) — Riya: "I trusted you. You were the only person I told."**
- "I know. I broke your trust and I feel terrible." → Trust +2 (very late recovery, relationship is fragile)
- "You shouldn't have told me if you didn't want anyone to know." → Trust -18 (relationship likely broken permanently)

#### Chain 4: The Test

Alex sees the answer sheet for tomorrow's Hindi test sitting on Priya Ma'am's desk when returning a notebook after class. No one else is around.

**Round 1 — The moment:**
- Walk away. Don't look at it. → Trust +6 (integrity when no one is watching)
- Glance at it quickly, just to see the format. → Trust -3 (rationalized peek)
- Take a photo of it with your phone. → Trust -10

**Round 2 (if took a photo) — Sam asks why Alex is so confident before the test:**
- "I just studied a lot." → Trust -4 (another lie stacks)
- Share the photo with Sam: "Look what I found." → Trust -12 (now two people are compromised)
- Delete the photo before the test: "Actually, I did something wrong. I'm going to study properly." → Trust +4 (self-correction)

**Round 3 (if shared with Sam) — Both get suspiciously high scores. Priya Ma'am notices and calls both of them:**
- Admit it: "I saw the answer sheet and took a photo. I shared it with Sam. It was wrong." → Trust -6 net (consequences but respect for honesty)
- Deny: "We just studied together." → Trust -14 (if caught later, consequences double)
- Blame Sam: "Sam asked me for answers, I didn't know where he got them." → Trust -20 (betrayal + dishonesty combo)

### End States for Tangled

**The Trusted Friend (80+)**
Alex finishes the term with strong friendships. Riya, Sam, and Kabir all trust Alex. Priya Ma'am writes a note home about Alex's character. The journal reads like a story of small brave choices. Message: "Trust is built one honest moment at a time. You built something real."

**Learning & Growing (50–79)**
Alex made mistakes but owned most of them. Some friendships took hits but recovered. Message: "Nobody's perfect. What matters is that when you stumbled, you chose to get back up. That takes courage too."

**Thin Ice (30–49)**
Relationships are strained. Riya is distant. Sam is cautious. Priya Ma'am is watching closely. Message: "Trust is like a rope — every lie cuts a thread. There's still rope left, but it's thin. What will you do tomorrow?"

**Tangled Up (below 30)**
Alex feels alone. Lies have piled up. Friends have pulled away. The journal reads like a cautionary tale. Message: "When you lie, you don't just fool others — you lose yourself in the tangle. But here's the thing: you can always start telling the truth again. It's never too late for the first honest word."

### Scenario Data Format

Each scenario chain is a data file, not hardcoded logic. This makes it easy to add new stories without touching the engine.

```javascript
// Example: games/tangled/scenarios/broken-phone.js
export default {
  id: "broken-phone",
  title: "The Broken Phone",
  setup: "It's lunch time. You're joking around with Sam when your elbow catches Riya's phone. It slides off the table and hits the floor. The screen is cracked.",
  rounds: [
    {
      id: "bp-1",
      prompt: "Riya picks up her phone. The screen is cracked. She looks at you.",
      choices: [
        {
          id: "own-up",
          text: "\"I'm so sorry, I knocked it. Let me talk to my parents about fixing it.\"",
          meterChange: +8,
          journal: "You told Riya the truth right away and offered to help fix her phone.",
          flags: { ownedUp: true },
          next: "bp-1-good"
        },
        {
          id: "deflect",
          text: "\"Oh no, it fell! I think it was already on the edge.\"",
          meterChange: -3,
          journal: "You didn't exactly lie, but you didn't take responsibility either.",
          flags: { deflected: true },
          next: "bp-2-deflect"
        },
        {
          id: "blame",
          text: "\"I didn't touch it — I think Sam bumped the table.\"",
          meterChange: -8,
          journal: "You blamed Sam for something you did. Sam looked confused.",
          flags: { blamed: true, blamedTarget: "sam" },
          next: "bp-2-blame"
        }
      ]
    },
    // ... subsequent rounds keyed by next IDs
  ]
}
```

### Key Engine Rules

- **Meter is global** — carries across all scenario chains within a game
- **Flags are cumulative** — scenarios can check flags from earlier chains (e.g., "if ownedUp in broken-phone, unlock bonus dialogue in the-secret")
- **Recovery multiplier** — consecutive bad choices increase the penalty multiplier (1x → 1.5x → 2x → 2.5x). A good choice resets the multiplier to 1x.
- **Threshold check** — when meter drops below 15 during a scenario, the engine marks that relationship/situation as "permanently damaged" and adjusts available choices in future rounds
- **Journal is append-only** — every choice adds an entry, creating a complete narrative of the playthrough

## Design & Tone Notes

- **Warm, not preachy.** The game never lectures. It shows consequences through story. The player feels the impact of their choices rather than being told they were wrong.
- **No "game over."** Even the worst ending is not a failure — it's a mirror. The game says "this is what happened" and asks "what would you do differently?"
- **Replayability is the lesson.** The most powerful moment is when a kid plays again and makes a different choice, and sees how the whole story changes. That's the ripple.
- **Culturally grounded.** Names, settings, and scenarios should feel familiar to Indian kids (school structure, teacher dynamics, family references) but universal enough for anyone.
- **Illustrations > realism.** Warm, slightly stylized illustrations — think picture book for older kids, not cartoon and not photorealistic.
- **Sound matters.** Subtle ambient sounds (school bell, playground noise, classroom hum) and gentle audio feedback on choices (a soft chime for good, a muted thud for bad) add emotional weight.
